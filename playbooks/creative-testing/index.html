Creative testing. How to find winning ads every week, not every quarter.

Adcrate ships 500+ ads a week for consumer brands and apps. Most of them lose. That’s normal. The job is to find the few that win before the account gets tired. This is our system, with the prompts, the templates and the numbers that made us change it.

01Weekly decisions, not weekly winners

Anyone can make 200 ads this afternoon. Claude writes the scripts, an image model makes the statics, and they’re in the ad account by dinner.

So volume isn’t an edge any more. Everyone has it.

Meta’s Andromeda update made it worse. It treats ads that look and sound alike as one. 10 headline swaps on the same angle, person and feeling count as one ad. You paid for 10 and tested 1.

The edge is knowing what to make and why. Then making it in volume.

Most ads lose. Across our accounts, roughly 3% to 22% of concepts become winners, depending on the brand and how you count. A client’s first real winner has taken 15 to 60 days. Anyone promising a winner every week is selling something.

What you can have every week is a decision. New ads go live on Friday. On Monday you read the results, decide what to kill, fund or iterate, and brief the next batch. Do that for a quarter and the winners come.

The system has 5 parts, each with a tool you can copy:

  1. Research that finds what customers really say.
  2. Angles that don’t overlap.
  3. Briefs that say what each ad must teach you.
  4. Testing you can read honestly.
  5. Scaling winners before they fade.

Formats get their own chapter, because everyone gets them wrong.

02Research: find the real story

Most ads are bad because they say what the brand would say. “Get rid of fine lines.” “Clinically proven.” “The best shampoo for dandruff.” Every competitor in the feed says the same thing.

Customers say different things. Better things.

A makeup brand for women over 50 could write “reduce fine lines and wrinkles”. Or it could lift a line straight from a review: “I don’t recognise myself in the mirror anymore.” One is a claim. The other is a feeling the reader had this morning.

Treat the brand as a guess and the ad account as evidence

Build 2 pictures and keep them apart.

First, the guess. Read the site, reviews and surveys. Write down who you think buys and why. Then read the ad account blind to that guess: what has spent and converted, and who it reached. Then compare.

ValidatedIn your guess and in the account. Double down.
DiscoveredIn the account but not your guess. A group is converting and nobody makes ads for them. Often the cheapest win in the account.
UntappedIn your guess but never tested. Fill the gap.

One warning: ads are aspirational. A group the account seems to reach is only a candidate until spend and sales back it up.

Where the words come from

In order: customer reviews (sort by longest, long positive ones hold the most emotion), post purchase surveys, ad comments, support tickets, Reddit, competitors’ bad reviews, organic TikTok. Then call 5 customers. Ask how they use it and why they bought. People avoid this step. It’s where the best angles come from.

Treat competitor ad libraries as context. Only look at ads that have run for a month or 2, and always click through to the landing page. An ad that works into a listicle can die on a normal product page.

3 things to look for

  • Trigger events. The moment someone decided they had to fix this. Ads that open on that moment and tell a story beat ads that go straight to problem and agitation.
  • High stakes. In reviews for a dandruff shampoo, we found a guy embarrassed because his girlfriend kept looking at the flakes on his shoulder. Dandruff is a nuisance. Dandruff that might cost you your girlfriend is urgent. Nobody in a strategy meeting would have written that hook. A customer did.
  • Edge cases. Customers who connected your product to a problem you never advertised. On a customer call for the same shampoo, a guy told us he uses it on his beard. A quick search showed plenty of people do. Nobody in the category was talking about beard dandruff. We made ads for it and they worked.

Group people by buying situation, not demographics

“Women 25 to 45” isn’t a persona. Neither is “gift buyers”, “mums” or “people who found us on TikTok”. Those are an age range, an action, a role and a channel.

2 groups are different only if they buy for a different reason, at a different moment or after trying different things first. The test: could a casting director hand you one recognisable person for this group? If not, keep going.

Ship 3 core groups, 4 at most. If the data only supports 2, ship 2.

Read it yourself

AI is brilliant at the first pass. We pull reviews, comments and surveys into one place (we built Parker for this) and have Claude find the stories. But read the raw material yourself. A summary can’t teach you how people talk. Reading line by line can.

And say out loud who the data overcounts. Reviews skew to happy, older, repeat buyers. Correct for it, or every brand looks like it sells to delighted 55 year olds.

Tool 1: the customer research prompt
You are a direct response researcher working for [BRAND], which sells [PRODUCT] to [MARKET].

Below is raw customer language: reviews, ad comments, post-purchase survey answers and support tickets. Work in two phases. Do not skip ahead.

PHASE 1: PATTERNS ONLY
List every repeated pattern you can find. For each one give:
- 2 to 3 verbatim quotes (never paraphrase), with the source type
- How many times it appears, as a rough count ("about 140 of 2,100 reviews")
Then pull out the 15 most emotionally loaded stories, tagged as:
- Trigger event: the moment someone decided they had to solve this
- High stakes: the problem threatened something bigger (a relationship, a job, how they see themselves)
- Edge case: someone using the product for something we don't advertise

PHASE 2: BUYING SITUATIONS
Using only language found in Phase 1, group buyers by buying situation. Two groups are different only if the reason for buying, the moment of buying or what they tried before is different.
Reject any group that is really an age range, an action (gifting), a role ("mums") or a channel.
For each group (3 core, 4 at most):
- A one-line description a casting director could cast
- The 2 quotes that best show what makes this group distinct
- Their main objection and what they tried before
- 3 first-line hooks written in their words, not ours

Finally: say who this data over-counts and under-counts, and list any source that came back empty.

03Angles that are actually different

Research gives you raw stories. Angles turn them into a plan. We use 4 layers so every ad in the account is there for a reason.

ThemeThe territory or problem space, not a product feature. 5 to 7 per brand, 2 to 3 active a month. Give each at least 4 weeks before you retire it.
AngleThe way into a theme, emotional or rational. An angle is a reason to buy, not a hook variation. 3 to 5 per theme.
FormatThe vehicle: UGC, AI animation, static, native, founder video. Pick the one that makes the angle hit hardest.
PersonaThe buying situation from your research. It shapes tone, casting and language.

Write every theme as one sentence you could be wrong about: “We believe [audience] struggles with [problem], and [product] solves it by [mechanism].” If it can’t hold 3 different angles, it’s an angle, not a theme.

Make each angle pull a different lever. Toward something they want. Away from something they fear. Curiosity. Social proof. Authority. Take a sleep supplement. One theme, 5 angles: the racing mind at 11pm, the 3am wake up, the melatonin hangover, work stress following you to bed, screens wrecking your sleep. 5 different ads, 5 different people nodding.

Do the maths before you make anything

1 theme × 5 angles × 4 formats × 3 personas = 60 structurally different ads. Don’t make all 60. Make 10 to 15, see which cells light up and build more inside those.

We tag every ad by format, persona, angle and emotion. A new ad with the same persona, angle and emotion as one already running is a duplicate, however different the headline looks. Ship into the gaps instead.

Keep about 20% of every batch for free swings: ideas the data can’t fully justify. Write a one line hypothesis for each so you learn something either way. Some of our best performers were ads the strategist didn’t believe in.

Angles that keep working

  • Borrowed authority. One of the best ads I’ve seen this year opens with a girl saying her grandpa fixed washing machines for a living. He leans in, sniffs her laundry and tells her she’s slow cooking her clothes in mould. The brand never claims anything. Grandpa does. Ask who would really know your problem and let them tell the story.
  • Us vs them. Still works. “You’ve probably tried [big brand]. Here’s how this is different.”
  • Moments. You don’t need Black Friday to create urgency. For a jeans brand we built ads around National Jeans Day in August. A Bible study brand ran a 90 day plan to read the whole Bible before the end of the year. Ask Claude for 20 moments your brand could own. Keep the ones with a real reason to buy now.
  • The underserved persona. Find what’s working for a big competitor, then go narrower. Same angle, aimed at a person they’re ignoring.

Be slow to call an angle dead. Low testosterone works. Look at the brands spending millions on it. If it failed for you, the execution failed. Change the format, persona or story before you bin the angle.

Judge angles on coverage, not ROAS. Angle ROAS is tangled up with persona, format and spend.

Tool 2: the angle gap map prompt
Below are (1) the buying situations from our research and (2) every ad that spent money in our account in the last 90 days, with spend and cost per result.

1. Tag each ad by buying situation, angle, format and emotion.
2. Build a grid: rows are buying situations, columns are angles, each cell lists the ads run and their combined spend.
3. Show customer voice share vs ad spend share for each buying situation.
4. Flag duplicates: ads with the same buying situation, angle and emotion that only differ by headline.
5. List the empty or thin cells. For each one write: the gap in one sentence, a test angle, your confidence (0 to 1) and why. Anything under 0.5 is a free swing, label it.

Then propose a batch of 10 concepts: about 80% filling gaps the data supports, about 20% free swings. Each concept needs a theme, angle, buying situation, format and the customer quote it's built on.

04Formats: the delivery, not the reason

People chase formats. Song ads, AI animation, drama ads. A new format can make a tired angle feel fresh. But if you don’t understand why it worked, you can’t repeat it.

The format carries the story. The story does the work.

If I could only run 2 formats for the rest of the year: AI animations and yappers, both built on storytelling.

AI animations

They show things that used to need an animator. What an ingredient does inside your body. What mould looks like inside a washing machine. The “I’m vitamin A and here’s what I do” style still works in big, product aware markets.

My favourite version takes a real trigger event from research and animates it as a story. Some run 5 to 10 minutes. Write the script as a song in Suno and have an editor animate to the music. Stories and songs make people feel something. That’s why they stick.

Don’t take it too far. “My wife left me, then I found this supplement and she came back” is a story. It’s also nonsense. Start from something a real customer said.

Yappers

A yapper is a creator who can tell a story and make you believe it. They’re worth far more than an average UGC creator.

Most brands think: a UGC video costs $150, so that’s what I pay. Wrong maths. A great storyteller might cost $300 to $600, or over $1,000 with a following. In an account spending $200k to $400k a month, one good yapper ad can spend hundreds of thousands of dollars. We now hunt for better, pricier creators on purpose. How we find them is in Creator programmes.

Statics

We build statics from templates: a cookbook of proven layouts with the strategy on top. The rules:

  • One job per ad. An ad that tries to do 2 jobs for 2 audiences does neither.
  • Pass the 1 second test. If a stranger can’t tell what’s sold and why in 1 second, kill it.
  • Be specific: a number, a timeframe or a named audience. Every number must be one you can back up.
  • Use real product photos. Never let the image model write the words. Render the image, then put the copy on in code.

Native or produced, never the middle

Decide once: does this ad look like content or like an ad? Then make everything match. A raw, confessional script with glossy hero shots and a slick end card is where ads go to die.

The cheapest diversity there is

Take a winning message and change the format. A static headline becomes a video hook. A video script becomes a native ad, a yapper or an animation. Same idea, and Meta treats it as a new ad.

05Briefs that teach you something

A brief has one job: make sure the person making the ad knows why it exists. Most briefs only say what to make. Then the ad loses and nobody learns anything.

Write the hypothesis

Every concept carries one sentence:

If we [do this creative thing], for [this person], by [tapping into this belief or insight], we expect [this result].

The test: if it doesn’t work, will we know why? “We’re testing testimonials” fails. “Emotion first peer reviews to win over sceptical first time buyers” passes.

Pick one metric before launch

A hook or angle test reads on hook rate or click through. A proof or offer test reads on cost per result or ROAS. Write it down before the ad goes live, with one question: what do we want to learn from this?

When it loses, ask the only useful question. Was the insight wrong, or the execution?

Make variations earn their place

Each concept gets up to 4 versions, each different for a reason:

AThe base version.
BSame person, different emotional lever (fear instead of curiosity, say).
CA different buying situation, or a fundamentally different hook.
DA big visual swing in the first 1 to 2 seconds.

If you can’t explain why a variation exists in 2 sentences, cut it.

A hook is a visual plus a line. Write 3 to 5 per concept, each a different way in.

Treat editors like strategists

Most brands treat editors like hands. Here are the clips, string them together, send it back.

Give them the context: why this ad exists, why this hook, which clips worked before. Give them view access to the ad account. Most editors have no idea how their ads perform. How are they meant to get better?

The good ones start pitching ideas. Some become strategists. Pay them a share of spend on ads they make end to end and they’ll make more on their own time.

Tool 3: the one page brief
CONCEPT CODE:      [brand code + number, e.g. JNS-041]
THEME / ANGLE:     [theme] / [angle in one sentence]
WHO:               [the buying situation, in plain words]
AWARENESS:         [unaware / problem aware / solution aware / product aware]
THE INSIGHT:       [the verbatim customer quote this is built on]

HYPOTHESIS:        If we [creative mechanic], for [person], by [belief or insight],
                   we expect [result].
WHAT WE'LL LEARN:  [one question this concept answers]
SUCCESS METRIC:    [one metric + target, e.g. cost per purchase within 10% of the
                   account after $300 spend]

FORMAT + STYLE:    [UGC / AI animation / static / native / founder] + [native or produced]
HOOKS (3 to 5):    [line] + [visual], one per row
STORY:             trigger moment > stakes > what they tried > product as the fix > proof > CTA
PROOF:             [reviews, numbers, authority. All real, all on file]
DON'T:             [claims we can't make, words the brand hates]

VARIATIONS:        A base / B new lever / C new person or hook / D new opening visual
                   One line each on why it exists.

06Testing you can read

Media buying matters less than people think. Meta is smart. Put an ad in the arena and it works out if it can scale. Your job is to give it a fair chance and read the result honestly.

The weekly rhythm

New ads go live every Friday. The monthly quota is split across the Fridays, so a brand getting 60 ads a month gets 15 a week, not 60 in week 4.

Go heavy in the first month: statics, recuts of footage the brand already owns, mashups, AI ads. A winner in the first batch is the best predictor we’ve found of how long a client stays. Most brands already have winners in their footage folder. What’s missing is the message.

A simple account structure

This works across lots of accounts:

Main campaignOne CBO for the considered, strategy led ads. Each concept in its own ad set with its 3 or so versions. Test and scale in the same place.
Volume campaignA separate CBO for raw content and big AI batches, so they don’t crowd out the considered ads.
Engagement heavyNatives and meme style ads get their own campaign. They pull so much engagement they skew everything around them.
GraveyardA CBO with a cost cap for ads that never got spend. It only spends when it can hit the target. Some ads are seasonal: black and orange nail polish does nothing in February and rips in October.

2 extra moves. If an ad hits a great cost per result but can’t pull spend, give it its own ad set and see if it scales alone. If an ad pulls spend at a bad cost per result, try it alone at a smaller budget.

Don’t overload the machine

Divide your daily budget by your target cost per result. That’s roughly how many ad sets Meta can learn from at once. $100 a day at a $30 target is about 3, not 30. Above $1,000 a day you’re usually fine.

A test that doesn’t spend isn’t a test

We once found our ads getting about 2% of a client’s budget. Everything sat in an automated campaign with no minimums, and the old ads ate it all. Weeks of “testing” had tested nothing. Now approved ads must be live within 7 days, and anything with no spend after 48 hours gets flagged.

Judge against the account, not the internet

Compare every ad with the same account, over the same window, on the event the account is optimised for. Purchases for a shop, sign ups for a newsletter, leads for lead gen. Twitter benchmarks mean nothing.

Judge the concept, not the single ad. Group every version that shares a concept code and read them together.

Make sure there’s enough to read. Our rule: at least 3 results, or spend of at least 1 account average cost per result. Anything less is “too early”. Don’t brief off it.

What a winner is

Our first winner rule used fixed numbers: $500 of weekly spend, at or below the account’s average cost. It called 15 of our 17 clients losers in a drought. The rule was wrong.

Now the rule is relative to each account. A winner is a concept that has spent at least $300 over 14 days, sits in the account’s top 10 by spend in each of the last 2 weeks, has a cost per result within 10% of the account’s, and isn’t getting worse week on week.

Sort every concept into one box, every week

BoxWhat it meansDo this
WinnerMeets the winner rule aboveFund it, then iterate it within 5 days

FadingWas a winner, last 7 days 20%+ worse than the 7 beforeRefresh it now: new hook, opener or creator

Read metrics in pairs

  • Strong hook, weak conversion: the CTA or the landing page isn’t matching the ad.
  • Weak hook, strong conversion: the right people love it, the opening visual is losing everyone else.
  • High click through, low conversion: a post click problem. Make the page repeat the ad’s promise.
  • Good hook, people drop off: keep the hook, rebuild the body.

Hook rate is a diagnostic. For a protein bar brand, our ads tripled the account’s hook rate, from 12% to 37%. Cost per purchase didn’t move. A 75% hook rate on the wrong people won’t outsell a 20% hook rate on the right ones.

Tool 4: the 30 minute Monday review
Below are last week's concepts with: concept code, days live, spend (7 and 14 days), results, cost per result, hook rate, hold rate, CTR, and the brief's success metric. Also the account's cost per result for the same window.

For each concept:
1. Check it got a fair chance: live within 7 days of approval and spending after 48 hours. If not, flag it as VOID and say why.
2. Put it in exactly one box, checking in this order:
   CUT: spent over 2x account cost per result with zero results
   TOO EARLY: fewer than 3 results and spend under 1x account cost per result
   FADING: was a winner, last 7 days 20%+ worse than previous 7
   WINNER: $300+ over 14 days, top 10 by spend both weeks, cost per result within 10% of account
   EARLY BUMP: 3 to 21 days old, cost at or below account, spend rising
   KILLED TOO EARLY: stopped before spending 1.5x account cost per result
3. For anything that missed its success metric, say whether the insight or the execution failed, using the metric pairs.

Then give me: what to fund, what to iterate (and the one thing to change), what to pull back, and the 3 questions next week's batch should answer.

CUT is a recommendation for a person to act on, never an automatic pause.

07Scaling winners before they fade

Most of the money is lost here, and it’s the last place you’d look.

In October 2026 we checked our own work. 12 of our 13 current winners had no iteration within 14 days. Winners fade, and if nothing is ready behind them, the account goes flat.

It wasn’t a tooling problem. We had an agent drafting iteration briefs. It was expensive and nobody used it. We switched it off and made a people rule: every winner gets an iteration in the database within 5 days, this week if it’s fading. Each client now gets 2 questions every Monday. Is there a winner? Are we iterating it?

Fund it before you edit it

If an efficient ad is starved of budget, fix the budget first. Don’t switch a winner off because you’ve copied it into another campaign. Turning off what works is the biggest mistake there is.

Iterate big, not small

Spend about 80% of your iteration time on big moves and 20% on small tests. Changing the music or one word rarely moves anything. These do, roughly in order:

  1. Change the format. Static headline to video hook to native to animation.
  2. Change the vehicle. Founder story, green screen, testimonial, listicle.
  3. Change the creator. Reshooting a winning script with a new creator has the highest success rate of any iteration we run.
  4. Change the hook, so Meta sees a new ad. 10 to 20 new hooks on a high spend winner.
  5. Raise the stakes. Same story, more urgent. “Slow cooking your clothes in mould” beats “bacteria in your washing machine.”

Keep everyday iterations cheap: keep what works, change one thing, use footage you already have. A reshoot with a new creator is worth it, but we count it as a new concept and plan it into the batch, so it doesn’t get lost in the iteration queue. 3 to 5 iterations per brand per month is about right. If there aren’t 3 real opportunities, call it a thin month and say why.

Don’t bin a concept after one creator. A winning script with the wrong creator will fail. Try 3 or 4 before you kill it.

Most iterations do worse than the parent. That’s fine. One newsletter’s flagship ad ran at $2.82 per sign up. Its iterations ran at $3.60 to $3.74. They still scaled spend, and the account learned what made the parent special.

Refresh a fading winner, don’t kill it

In September 2026 one winner’s cost per purchase went from $73 to $119 in a single week, about 60% worse, and nobody flagged it. Now anything that was a winner in the last 30 days and gets 20% worse week on week is flagged as fading. Refresh it before it dies: new hook, new opener, new creator or a shorter cut.

Move winners across surfaces

  • Landing pages. A winning ad deserves its own page. See Landing pages for ads.
  • Whitelisting. Run the winner from the creator’s own account.
  • Placements. Recut 9:16 winners for 1:1 and 16:9.

Close the loop every week

Every week we sort what won and what lost, and Claude proposes a one line learning for each. A person approves every learning before it goes anywhere near a prompt. Approved learnings shape the next batch. The rest get deleted. Skip approval and your system confidently learns the wrong lessons.

Be stricter still across brands. A learning only becomes a pattern once it has won in at least 3 brands across at least 2 categories. Visual style, exact copy and product positioning never travel.

Tool 5: the winner iteration brief
WINNER:          [concept code] · [spend last 14 days] · [cost per result vs account]
STATUS:          winner / fading (last 7 days vs previous 7: [+x%])
WHY IT WINS:     [one line: the insight, hook, person, format or offer doing the work]

STEP 1 FUND:     Is it starved of budget? If yes, fix that first and stop here.
STEP 2 DIAGNOSE: Weakest link:
                 hook rate low      > new hook or opening visual
                 hold low           > pacing or a tighter body
                 CTR low            > CTA, offer or headline
                 frequency high + cost rising > format swap, new opener, cut-down
STEP 3 ONE MOVE: [the single thing we're changing], from footage we already have
                 (new shoot or creator needed? Then it's a new concept, not an iteration)
STEP 4 WRITE:    [new hook / new edit notes / new headline]
STEP 5 NUMBER:   [base code].[next number]   e.g. JNS-041.3 (never JNS-041.2.1)
STEP 6 LOG:      In 14 to 21 days: beat / matched / lost the base, and the one-line learning.

RULES: never repeat a change that already lost on this concept. Never iterate an ad
that only wins because of a promo.

08Where AI fits, and where it doesn’t

AI is the best thing to happen to marketers in a long time. It also exposes the bad ones.

Everyone has the same tools. The output depends on the context you give them. A great marketer knows why a hook works and why that clip belongs in the first second. A weak one doesn’t, so their AI doesn’t either.

AI proposes. A named person approves. Nothing goes out on its own.

What we’ve learned:

  • Volume with intent. Our static workflow can make hundreds of ads, but it starts by reading the account: which buying situations we’ve tested, which we haven’t, what customers keep saying. Volume pointed at a gap is useful. Volume pointed at nothing is spam.
  • Examples help, then hurt. “Recreate this winning script” beats “write me a script.” But when we loaded our tools with example winners, they kept suggesting the same ideas. Give it examples. Don’t let it copy them.
  • AI can’t write good scripts cold. Start from a reference ad and real customer words. Our strategists rewrite every script by hand.
  • Commentate while you work. Strategists and editors talk through why they do what they do, and we record it. Those transcripts become the context that makes agents good later.
  • Never invent a number. Any statistic in an ad has to already exist in the brand’s own material. If there’s no number on file, write the claim without one.

Let AI read, sort, draft and watch. Keep people on the decisions: what to test, what counts as a win, what ships.

09Mistakes we’ve made

  • Counting variations as tests. We used to report 10 headlines on one angle as 10 tests. It’s 1.
  • A winner rule with fixed numbers. Measure each account against itself.
  • Tests that never spent. Check spend before you read results.
  • Celebrating hook rate. We tripled it. Cost per purchase didn’t move.
  • Not iterating winners. The fix was a rule, not a tool.
  • Letting research go stale. Our best angles came from a customer call or a comment we read ourselves.

Run more different ideas. Judge them against your own account. Pile into the winners before they fade. Then do it again next Friday.
