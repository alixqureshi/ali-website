Playbook 03 · 10 min read · Updated 20 Oct 2026

Creator programmes. How to find, brief and pay creators at scale, without a team of 4.

Adcrate has a pool of over 3,000 creators and more than 8,000 ad concepts linked to them. The creator team used to be 4 people. Now it’s 1.5, plus a set of agents that do the admin. This is the whole system, including the parts that broke.

01Creator work is admin work

Managing creators is mostly chasing.

Chasing replies. Chasing contracts. Chasing invoices. Chasing footage that was due on Tuesday. Answering the same 20 questions about rates, deadlines and file formats. Our creator team spent most of the day in WhatsApp. We had more than 300 group chats, set up only so the rest of the team could follow along.

The money was messy too. In June, roughly 20% of creator spend wasn’t tracked against any brand. Creator costs ran at about 10% of revenue, and nobody saw them until the month closed. Around 80% of creators only ever did 1 concept with us. We kept paying to onboard strangers.

Agents are good at admin. They’re bad at judgement. The whole system is built on that split.

02The pool: one database, one row per creator

Everything starts with one database. Ours lives in Notion. One row per creator, forever. Creators apply through a form (US and UK versions) and each one becomes a row. The team can also add someone by hand with a name and a portfolio link.

What each row holds

BasicsName, contact, country, age range, gender (couples count), shipping address.
SearchableNiche, what they shoot, tags like “50+ creator” or “couple creator”.
CommercialRate, rate status, signed or not, source (inbound, scouted, referral).
JudgementTier, rating, recommended, performance notes. Only people fill these in.
StatusProposed › To contact › Contacted › Applied › Active. Side exits for no response, declined, inactive and blacklisted.

Let an agent fill in the boring fields

Nobody wants to open 3,000 portfolios and type in a niche. So an agent does it. Every 4 hours, our Pool Enricher opens new portfolios in a real browser, because lots of creators use Canva sites a simple scraper can’t read. It works out niche, age range, country and what they shoot, grabs a headshot and tidies the links. If it can’t read one, it tags the row “check manually” and moves on.

It backfilled 2,251 rows in one go, on a small, cheap model, for pennies. You don’t need the smartest model for data entry.

It never touches the judgement fields. It once declined 3 creators who had already done approved work for us. Now every decline waits for a person.

03Casting: honest shortlists, fast

Casting starts with a plain request from a strategist, in a requests database or straight from Slack: “Women 45+, US, dry skin, natural light, can do a talking head.”

Our Casting agent then does 2 things:

  1. Searches the pool. It maps the brief to pool fields and shortlists up to 5 signed, active creators.
  2. Searches outside it. It finds new people on creator marketplaces (Collabstr, JoinBrands, Influee, Fiverr and others) and tags each as a strong fit, possible fit or long shot.

The shortlist lands in Slack and in a comment that tags the strategist.

The must-have rule

Every brief has one requirement that defines it. For an ad about cracked heels, it’s a creator with cracked heels. Everything else is nice to have.

Early versions padded the list with near misses to look helpful. That wasted more time than no list at all. Now that requirement is a must-have, and the agent says “no match” when nobody fits. If the pool can’t record the must-have (acne, a dog, cracked heels), it shortlists the closest people with a question to ask them first.

An honest “no match” beats a padded shortlist.

Keep your best creators busy

Aim for about 3 creators per brand a month: 1 new and 2 you’ve used before. Repeat creators need less briefing and already know the product. A weekly agent flags our premium creators: anyone who’s done repeat work, whose ads performed, or who strategists liked. That list, around 80 people right now, is who we call first.

Pay the good ones properly. A creator who can tell a story is worth 2 to 4 times an average one, because their ads can spend for months. More on that in Creative testing.

Steal this: the casting prompt
You cast UGC creators for paid social ads. Below is a creator request and an export of our creator pool.

1. Read the request and name the ONE must-have requirement (the thing the ad is impossible without). List everything else as nice to have.
2. Translate the request into pool fields: country, age range, gender, niche, what they shoot, tags.
3. From the pool, shortlist up to 5 creators who are Signed and Active and meet the must-have. For each: name, why they fit, any nice-to-haves they miss, last brand they worked on.
4. If nobody meets the must-have, say "No match" clearly. Do not pad the list.
5. If the must-have can't be recorded in the pool (a skin condition, a pet, a body type), shortlist the 5 closest creators and write one short question to confirm it with each.
6. Suggest where to search outside the pool, and what to search for.

Never invent creators, handles or rates.

04Briefs creators can follow

A brief should let a creator film without messaging us. Ours are built from the strategist’s concept and cover:

  • The concept in one sentence and the script, with lines they must say word for word and lines they can make their own.
  • Every shot they need: hook options, talking head, B-roll, product close-ups.
  • Setting, lighting and what to wear.
  • The due date. Ours is 5 to 7 days after the product arrives, and 1 to 2 weeks before the client needs the ad.
  • How to name the files. We forgot this for months, so checking footage by file name never worked.
  • Where to upload. One folder per concept.

Share briefs as private links. Public Notion pages nearly got our workspace switched off.

An agent drafts the brief and message. A person approves the exact wording before it goes.

05One inbox for every creator

Our biggest fix: every creator conversation in one inbox.

We use Chatwoot, on our own server, branded as our Creator Dashboard. We picked it over Front and Missive because there’s no fee per seat. The whole team sees every chat for the cost of a small server. It has 3 inboxes:

EmailOne shared creators@ address. Invoices go here too.
WhatsAppA dedicated number on Meta’s official API. Direct chats with each creator, not hundreds of groups.
iMessageA bridge on a Mac mini that never switches off. US creators answer blue bubbles far more than WhatsApp.

Every new chat goes to the creator lead automatically. 21 saved replies cover the common questions. 12 approved WhatsApp templates start new conversations.

The agent drafts. A person sends.

When a creator messages us, our Creator Comms agent reads the chat, checks their booking and drafts a reply as a private note, in our creator lead’s voice, based on her real replies. One model writes it, a second checks it.

A person reads each draft, edits if needed and hits send. Right now about 1 in 4 goes out almost word for word. That number decides when it’s ready for more.

A separate Slack bot answers the team’s questions about creators (“has she signed?”, “when’s her footage due?”), capped at $25 a month.

2 things that caught us out

Meta blocks WhatsApp marketing templates to US numbers. So cold outreach to US creators goes by iMessage.

We once had 2 drafting agents running at the same time. 25 of 34 messages got 2 different replies. One agent per job.

06Contracts and the paper trail

Every creator signs before they film. The creator lead asks for a contract in Slack, an agent drafts it in PandaDoc, she checks the actual document, and it goes out. Once signed, an agent marks the creator signed and active in the pool. Most come back within hours. If one isn’t signed in 3 days, we chase once, then cancel.

The terms that matter:

  • What they deliver: raw clips for each concept, B-roll and a talking head take. We write the brief and script, and we edit.
  • How they’re paid: a flat fee per video, paid in full once we approve it.
  • Whitelisting: an optional monthly fee if we run ads from their account. We can stop it any time.
  • Revisions: one round included. A new concept is paid separately.
  • Late delivery: 50% off after the first deadline, 75% off after the second.
  • Usage: a full licence to use the content in ads, in all media.
  • No going around us: creators don’t deal with the brand directly. They can post the work to their portfolio about 2 weeks after delivery, with approval.
  • Housekeeping: copyright free audio only, and keep the product for 2 weeks in case we need a reshoot.

Get a lawyer to write yours. If it misses any of these, you’ll find out why the hard way.

07Paying and chasing

Every booking moves through the same stages, and every stage has a date:

Ship product › Shipping › Filming › Submitted (or missing clips) › Waiting for invoice › Make payment › Paid

Every 15 minutes, a small script stamps the date on any booking that moved. That one change made late work visible. The creator dashboard shows 4 numbers every morning: creators waiting more than 24 hours for a reply, overdue requests, contracts waiting more than a day, and footage past its due date.

Payment is boring on purpose. Once clips are approved, the creator sends an invoice to creators@. We pay every Friday. In our accounting system (Xero), one “Creators” account is the only source of truth for creator spend. Finance only steps in on exceptions.

Make the client fund the creators

Since September 2026, every new client pays for their own creator budget. We lowered our fee slightly to make room. Before, creator costs came out of our margin. Now the budget is agreed up front and the client sees what it buys. Whitelisting fees pass straight through.

08From footage to ads

An agent watches each concept folder for new footage, waits 45 minutes for uploads to finish, then checks it against the brief. Are all the shots there? Did they say the required lines? If something’s missing, it drafts a reshoot request for the creator lead to approve.

It’s still in testing. A person reviews every upload until it gets 9 out of 10 right.

Most brands skip this: link every ad back to the creator. Every concept has a code, every ad name carries it, and every concept links to its creators. Search the ad account and you can see which creators drive spend. If one creator carries a big share of your UGC spend, stop treating her like a supplier. Give her more work, pay her more, maybe bring her in house.

The go-live rule

No agent talks to a creator just because it worked in a demo. To go live, it needs at least 5 real decisions in 7 days, with 80% or more approved without edits. Then it’s checked against the source records, reviewed by a second model and signed off by the creator lead. Anything that messages creators also needs my yes.

As of this week, nothing goes to creators automatically. We switched off every automatic send after a re-engagement agent emailed 27 creators saying “we haven’t worked together in a while”. All 27 had unpaid bookings with us. Embarrassing. That’s why the rules exist.

Automate the routine. Keep people on anything that touches money or judgement.

Rates, buyouts, late penalties, reshoots and casting stay with people. That’s about a third of the work. The rest is admin, and the agents take it.

10If you’re starting from zero

Don’t start with agents. Start with the boring structure they’ll run on.

  1. One pool. A database with one row per creator and the fields above, fed by an application form.
  2. One inbox. Move every conversation out of personal WhatsApp and into a shared inbox.
  3. One contract and one brief template. Same terms, same structure, every time.
  4. Booking stages with dates. So you can see what’s late without asking anyone.
  5. Codes that link ads to creators. So you know who’s worth paying more.
  6. Then agents, one at a time, starting with data entry. Draft first, send later. Every agent earns the right to act alone.

Agents only work when the structure under them is clean. Build that first.
